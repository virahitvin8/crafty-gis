                     Timely and accurate diagnosis of plant diseases is essential to minimizing crop dam-

                   Among the various indicators of plant health, leaf symptoms remain the most visible

                   Despite this, Egypt suffers from a noticeable shortage of agricultural extension person-

                   opened new frontiers in the development of automated plant disease detection systems.
                   The proliferation of smartphones and the accessibility of GPU-powered computing
                   have further facilitated the deployment of such technologies in real-world agricultural
                   contexts. Among various AI techniques, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have
                   exhibited remarkable efficacy in image-based classification tasks, including plant dis-
                   ease identification. Several studies have demonstrated the successful application of CNN
                   architectures such as VGG16, InceptionV3, DenseNet201, and MobileNetV2, achieving

                   and visualizing spatially referenced data. GIS integrates hardware, software, and analyti-
                   cal methodologies to support the geospatial representation of phenomena, including the
                   spread of plant diseases [5]. By enabling the construction of spatial distribution maps,
                   GIS contributes to identifying disease hot-spots, monitoring crop health dynamics, and
                   informing precision agriculture practices [6]. The integration of GIS with real-time plant
                   disease diagnostics offers the potential to support early warning systems and enhance
                   evidence-based agricultural planning and interventions.
                     Despite these parallel advancements, current research efforts often treat deep learn-
                   ing-based diagnosis and GIS applications as distinct domains. Most AI-driven plant dis-
                   ease detection models are limited to classifying disease symptoms from images, lacking
                   spatial contextualization or actionable insights for decision support. Conversely, GIS
                   applications in agriculture frequently rely on manually reported data and rarely incor-
                   porate automated, real-time diagnostic outputs. This gap underscores the need for an
                   integrated approach that combines deep learning and GIS to enhance both the accuracy
                   of disease detection and the spatial intelligence of agricultural decision-making.

                   2 Literature review
                   2.1 Deep learning in plant disease diagnosis
                   Deep learning has revolutionized plant disease diagnosis by enabling automated,
                   image-based recognition of visual symptoms on leaves. Over the past decade, CNNs
                   have emerged as the dominant architecture due to their hierarchical feature extraction
Maged et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence   (2026) 6:344                                                      Page 4 of 33




                   capabilities and high classification accuracy. Existing research on CNN-based diagnosis
                   can be conceptually grouped into three major themes: (1) model architecture and trans-
                   fer learning approaches, (2) hybrid and optimized network designs, and (3) lightweight
                   and mobile-oriented architectures for field deployment.

                      (1) Model Architecture and Transfer Learning Approaches.
                          Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of pretrained CNNs for plant
                          disease recognition using benchmark datasets such as PlantVillage. For instance [8],
                          achieved 98.75% test accuracy, while [9] compared VGG16, InceptionV3, ResNet152V2,
                          and DenseNet201, identifying DenseNet201 as the top performer (99.30% validation
                          accuracy). Similarly, Too [10] reported that DenseNet121 outperformed other
                          architectures with 99.75% accuracy on 54,306 images, reinforcing the effectiveness of
                          deep transfer learning in plant pathology.
                      (2) Hybrid and Optimized Network Designs.
                          Recent research has advanced beyond conventional CNNs toward hybrid and
                          ensemble architectures that enhance feature representation and robustness. In [11],
                          a Region-based Convolutional Neural Network–Region-based Fully Convolutional
                          Network (RCNN–RFCN) hybrid model achieved 98.5% accuracy, while [12] proposed
                          UnitedModel, a combined CNN framework that reached 99.17% accuracy for grape
                          leaf disease detection. These hybridization strategies reflect a growing trend toward
                          model fusion for improved generalization and contextual understanding.
                       (3) Lightweight and Mobile-Oriented Architectures.
                          Given the demand for real-time diagnosis in agricultural environments, lightweight
                          CNNs have gained increasing attention. In [13] employed depthwise separable
                          convolutions to reduce computational cost while maintaining 99.56% accuracy
                          across 38 diseases, making the model suitable for mobile deployment. Similarly [14],
                          found that ShuffleNet achieved a reasonable 83.68% accuracy with significantly fewer
                          parameters, illustrating the balance between model complexity and efficiency.

                   Despite these advancements, several studies in [15] highlight ongoing challenges related
                   to dataset variability, background noise, and environmental conditions that hinder
                   model generalization beyond controlled datasets such as PlantVillage. Therefore, inte-
                   grating deep learning with contextual and geospatial tools like GIS represents a crucial
                   step toward developing field-ready, intelligent decision-support systems for sustainable
                   agriculture.

                   2.2 GIS in plant disease monitoring and management
                   GIS and Remote Sensing (RS) technologies have become indispensable tools in modern
                   plant disease monitoring and management, providing spatial and temporal insights that
                   enhance early detection, forecasting, and intervention. Existing research in this area can
                   be grouped into three major thematic domains: (1) environmental and epidemiologi-
                   cal mapping, (2) precision agriculture and decision-support systems, and (3) geospatial
                   forecasting and sustainability applications.
                     (1) Environmental and Epidemiological Mapping.
                     Several studies have demonstrated how GIS and RS facilitate the visualization and
                   analysis of environmental factors driving plant disease dynamics. In [16] integrated GIS
                   and RS to map disease–environment interactions across multiple crops, enabling early
Maged et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence   (2026) 6:344                                                        Page 5 of 33




                   detection of wheat yellow rust, rice sheath blight, and potato late blight. Similarly [17],
                   employed high-resolution satellite imagery and Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) data to
                   assess spatial variability in crop health, illustrating how geospatial layers can reveal early
                   symptoms before visible damage occurs.
                     (2) Precision Agriculture and Decision-Support Systems.
                     The use of GIS-based mobile and web platforms for real-time agricultural monitoring
                   has expanded rapidly [18]. developed an Android-based GIS application for pest and dis-
                   ease monitoring, demonstrating strong reliability and user adoption [19]. reviewed GIS–
                   RS advancements in oil palm management, emphasizing the integration of machine
                   learning and deep learning to optimize precision agriculture workflows. Likewise [20],
                   demonstrated the use of GIS, Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS), and hyper-
                   spectral remote sensing within precision agriculture frameworks for targeted disease
                   management, achieving reduced pesticide application and improved decision efficiency.
                     (3) Geospatial Forecasting and Sustainable Management.
                     Forecasting models using GIS have proven valuable for proactive disease control and
                   sustainable planning [21, 22]. highlighted the role of geospatial forecasting in develop-
                   ing disease-resistant crop strategies and timely interventions. Previous studies demon-
                   strated how GIS–RS integration supports early detection and large-scale surveillance,
                   while [23, 24] emphasized its capacity to assess both biotic and abiotic stressors influ-
                   encing plant health and productivity.
                     Collectively, these studies confirm GIS and RS as vital components of plant dis-
                   ease management ecosystems. However, most implementations remain crop-specific,
                   regional, and detached from AI-driven diagnosis systems. This gap underscores the need
                   for integrated DL–GIS frameworks capable of combining image-based diagnosis with
                   spatial and environmental intelligence to enable predictive, data-informed agricultural
                   decision-making.

                   2.3 Integration of deep learning and GIS in agriculture
                   While DL demonstrates strong capabilities in classifying plant diseases from leaf images
                   and GIS excel in spatial analysis, their integration in real-time agricultural decision-
                   support systems remains limited. DL models typically lack spatial and temporal context,
                   whereas GIS often depends on manually collected or delayed data. By combining the
                   two, static image-based diagnoses can be transformed into dynamic, location-aware
                   insights that enable visualization of disease hotspots, detection of temporal trends, and
                   more efficient resource allocation.
                     Although some recent studies, such as Suresh et al. [7], have applied deep learning
                   (DL) for plant disease monitoring, most approaches remain experimental or limited in
                   scope, often lacking real-time functionality or large-scale validation. This underscores a
                   critical research gap, which the present study addresses by developing a unified DL–GIS
                   framework specifically designed for the diagnosis, mapping, and management of tomato
                   and potato diseases.

                   2.4 Summary and research gap
                   The reviewed literature confirms that CNN-based deep learning models achieve
                   high diagnostic accuracy, while GIS and remote sensing play critical roles in spatial
                   analysis and disease monitoring. However, these two domains have largely evolved

                   independently, limiting the delivery of real-time, spatially aware decision-support sys-
                   tems. Deep learning models typically lack geospatial context, and GIS-based monitoring
                   systems often depend on delayed or manually collected data.
                     To address this research gap, this study proposes an integrated deep learning–GIS
                   framework for intelligent plant disease management. Three models—DenseNet169,
                   MobileNetV2, and a Custom-CNN—were comparatively evaluated, with DenseNet169
                   demonstrating the best performance (98.24% accuracy). Accordingly, DenseNet169 was
                   selected for GIS integration to visualize disease hotspots and analyze potential spread
                   dynamics using environmental parameters.
                     This integration enables automatic mapping of diagnosed cases, temporal tracking of
                   outbreaks, and early identification of high-risk zones. By linking diagnostic intelligence
                   with geospatial analytics, the proposed system contributes a practical, scalable, and
                   sustainable solution to enhance agricultural resilience and support data-driven disease
                   management.

                   3 Methodology
                   3.1 Overview of the proposed model
                   In this study, three deep learning models were developed to classify healthy and dis-
                   eased tomato and potato leaves using the PlantVillage dataset with a 70/15/15 split for
                   training, validation, and testing. The models included two transfer learning architec-
                   tures—DenseNet169 and MobileNetV2—alongside a Custom-CNN built from scratch.
                   Experimental results showed that DenseNet169 achieved the highest accuracy (98.24%),
                   followed by MobileNetV2 (94.66%), while the Custom-CNN recorded a lower accuracy
                   (82.60%) due to its shallow depth and limited feature extraction capability.
                     Based on these results, DenseNet169 was selected for integration with the GIS mod-
                   ule, as it provided superior accuracy and robustness, making it suitable for precise and
                   reliable deployment.
                     The proposed framework integrates DenseNet169-based diagnosis with GIS, enabling
                   both automated classification and spatial visualization of disease outbreaks. CNN archi-
                   tectures effectively capture hierarchical features from leaf images, while transfer learning
                   accelerates convergence and improves generalization. The overall pipeline combines pre-
                   processing, prediction, and geospatial mapping to deliver a scalable and practical deci-
                   sion-support system for agriculture (Fig. 1).
                     Recent works have emphasized the value of transfer learning and spatially enabled
                   deep-learning frameworks in agricultural image analysis. For example, In [25] proposed
                   an automated annotation and segmentation framework for multi-date aerial image anal-
                   ysis, demonstrating how deep neural networks can extract spatially meaningful patterns
                   from agricultural imagery. Inspired by this line of research, the present study adopts a
                   similar deep-learning-driven approach for spatially referenced disease detection, inte-
                   grating CNN-based diagnosis with GIS visualization to enhance interpretability and
                   decision support.

                   for potato), covering both healthy and diseased samples such as Early Blight, Late Blight,

                   3.3 Image preprocessing
                   To ensure consistency and optimize model performance, all images were resized from
                   256 × 256 to 224 × 224 pixels, aligning with the input requirements of the selected CNN-
                   based architectures (DenseNet169, MobileNetV2, and a Custom-CNN). Pixel values
                   were normalized to the range [0, 1] by dividing each RGB channel by 255, which stabi-
                   lized the learning process, standardized input distributions, and facilitated faster conver-
                   gence. These preprocessing steps effectively conditioned the dataset for robust training
                   and evaluation across the four models.

                   3.4 Augmentation process
                   To enhance CNN robustness and mitigate overfitting, data augmentation was applied
                   using TensorFlow’s ImageDataGenerator. Transformations included horizontal and ver-
                   tical flips, random rotations (± 20°), zooming (± 20%), width and height shifts, shearing,
Maged et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence    (2026) 6:344                                                    Page 8 of 33




                   Fig. 2 Presents representative samples from the 13 tomato and potato classes

                   brightness adjustments, and rescaling, simulating natural variations in leaf orientation,
                   size, and lighting conditions. Augmentation was applied consistently for DenseNet169,
                   MobileNetV2, and a Custom-CNN, while a lighter augmentation pipeline was adopted
                   for the custom CNN to reduce training complexity. This approach contributed to
                   improved generalization and stability across all evaluated models.

                   3.5 Dataset partitioning and evaluation strategy
                   The dataset was partitioned using a stratified 70:15:15 split, resulting in 12,709 images
                   for training, 2,719 for validation, and 2,735 for testing, with balanced representation
                   across all 13 classes. This ensured fair evaluation and prevented data leakage between
                   the training, validation, and testing sets. The same partitioning strategy was applied con-
                   sistently across all three models: DenseNet169, MobileNetV2, and Custom-CNN.
                     Early stopping was implemented during training to prevent overfitting and improve
                   generalization. Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics, including
                   Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and Support, complemented by confusion matri-
                   ces for class-specific evaluation. Training and validation curves were monitored to track
                   convergence, while ROC curves and AUC scores provided additional insight into each
                   model’s discriminative capability across disease classes.
                     This evaluation framework enabled a robust comparison of the three architectures,
                   highlighting their relative trade-offs in terms of accuracy, efficiency, and practical
                   deployment suitability.

                   3.6 Fine-tuning strategy
                   Fine-tuning was performed by optimizing key hyperparameters such as batch size, opti-
                   mizer, learning rate, and validation strategy [10]. For DenseNet169 and MobileNetV2,
                   training was conducted in two stages: 20 frozen epochs followed by 20 unfrozen epochs,
                   allowing gradual adaptation of pre-trained weights. In contrast, the Custom-CNN was
                   trained in a single stage of 40 epochs. Early stopping was applied consistently across all
                   models to prevent overfitting and ensure fair comparison under unified experimental
                   conditions.

                   3.7 Model comparison strategy
                   To ensure an objective and reproducible assessment of model performance, the three
                   architectures—DenseNet169, MobileNetV2, and the Custom-CNN—were trained and
                   evaluated under identical experimental conditions, using the same data splits, augmen-
                   tation settings, and optimization parameters. The comparative analysis focused on accu-
                   racy, loss, and generalization ability across the test dataset.
                     To statistically validate whether the observed performance differences were signifi-
                   cant, McNemar’s test was applied for pairwise model comparisons. This non-parametric
                   test evaluates the degree of agreement and disagreement between two classifiers on the
                   same dataset, offering a robust framework for assessing comparative performance reli-
                   ability [26]. This statistical evaluation approach aligns with recent methodological stan-
                   dards for comparative deep learning model validation, which emphasize the integration
                   of significance testing to ensure that observed performance differences are not due to
                   random variation [27].

                   3.8 Training the models
                   Three models were trained for tomato and potato disease classification: DenseNet169,
                   MobileNetV2, and a Custom-CNN. The Custom-CNN was designed with three convo-
                   lutional blocks followed by fully connected layers, while the transfer learning models
                   (DenseNet169 and MobileNetV2) adopted a unified classification head during fine-tun-
                   ing. All models were trained using the same dataset partitioning and evaluation strategy
                   described earlier, ensuring consistency in comparison.

                   3.9 Feature extraction and Explain-ability
                   The three models adopted distinct feature extraction strategies. DenseNet169 utilized
                   dense connectivity across layers, facilitating efficient feature reuse and enabling the
                   extraction of highly discriminative representations for fine-grained disease classification.
                   MobileNetV2 employed depth-wise separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks
                   with linear bottlenecks, producing compact yet expressive feature maps that are particu-
                   larly suitable for lightweight deployment in real-time systems. In contrast, the Custom-
                   CNN, although less accurate, learned hierarchical features from scratch, progressing
                   from low-level edges and textures to higher-level symptomatic leaf structures.
                     To enhance interpretability, Grad-CAM was applied to DenseNet169 and Mobile-
                   NetV2, producing class-discriminative heatmaps that highlight the leaf regions most rel-
                   evant to model predictions. These visualizations validated that the models consistently
                   attend to disease-affected areas, thereby reinforcing their reliability and transparency in
                   practical applications. Similar applications of Grad-CAM in plant pathology have dem-
                   onstrated its effectiveness in confirming model focus on symptomatic regions in grape
                   leaves [28] and in pest detection frameworks [29]. Representative examples from our
                   study are presented in Fig. 3 (sample from the training dataset) and Fig. 4 (field sam-
                   ple from Badr Village, Beheira, January 2025), illustrating both accuracy and robustness
                   under real conditions.
                     These visual comparisons validated the model’s transferability and practical relevance
                   in real agricultural environments.

                   3.10 GIS-Driven disease monitoring and environmental integration
                   3.10.1 Role of GIS in plant disease management
                   Geographic Information Systems (GIS) provide a spatial framework for analyzing the
                   distribution of plant diseases and their environmental determinants. Because disease
                   outbreaks are strongly influenced by climatic and ecological factors, GIS facilitates early
                   detection, continuous monitoring, and the design of targeted intervention strategies.
                   Previous studies have demonstrated that integrating GIS with AI-based diagnostic mod-
                   els enhances the tracking and prediction of plant diseases, particularly for potato late
                   blight and viral infections in tomato crops.
                     In this study, GIS was combined with deep learning models to map the spatial occur-
                   rence of tomato and potato diseases across different regions. This integration enabled
                   not only accurate disease diagnosis at the leaf level but also the visualization of disease
                   spread patterns at both field and regional scales. By linking diagnosis with geospatial
                   insights, the framework provides stronger decision-support tools for farmers and poli-
                   cymakers, promoting rapid interventions to mitigate large-scale outbreaks.

                   3.10.2 GIS-Based visualization and Multi-Crop layering
                   The integration of DenseNet169 outputs into an interactive GIS platform was achieved
                   using Leaflet.js, enabling real-time visualization of disease occurrences. Each diagnostic
                   result was geotagged and displayed on the map as a color-coded marker: red for tomato
                   diseases and brown for potato diseases. To enhance interpretability, the system supports
                   multi-layer visualization, allowing users to toggle crop-specific layers independently.
                   This functionality provides a clear spatial overview of disease dynamics across crops,
                   facilitates comparative analysis, and supports targeted management interventions at
                   both farm and regional levels.
Maged et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence   (2026) 6:344                                                      Page 11 of 33




                   3.10.3 Diagnosis logging and metadata recording
                   All disease predictions generated by DenseNet169 are automatically logged within
                   the system together with detailed metadata, including the confidence score, detection
                   timestamp, crop type, GPS coordinates, and key environmental parameters (e.g., tem-
                   perature, humidity, and soil moisture). This structured logging framework ensures full
                   traceability of diagnostic outputs and provides a reliable basis for historical trend analy-
                   sis across different seasons and geographic regions. By capturing both spatial and tem-
                   poral dimensions, the recorded data support advanced epidemiological studies, enabling
                   the modeling of disease spread dynamics, identification of high-risk areas, and evalua-
                   tion of intervention outcomes. Furthermore, this metadata-driven approach strength-
                   ens data-informed agricultural decision-making, offering actionable insights for farmers,
                   policymakers, and researchers to enhance crop health monitoring and implement sus-
                   tainable disease management strategies.

                   3.10.4 Integration of environmental data and spread prediction
                   To enhance the predictive capability of the proposed framework, real-time environmen-
                   tal parameters—namely temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), wind speed (m/s), and
                   general weather conditions—were dynamically retrieved using the OpenWeatherMap
                   API. These parameters were incorporated into a disease spread model designed to esti-
                   mate the potential infection radius based on localized climatic variations [30, 31].
                     The predicted spread radius (R) for each detected disease case was computed as fol-
                   lows (Eq. 1):

                          R = B × (1 + α · ∆T) × (1 + β · H) × (1 + γ · W) × δ (1)

                   Where: B: Base radius (5 km) [32], ΔT: Normalized temperature deviation [33], H:
                   Humidity factor [31], W: Wind speed factor [31], δ: Disease-specific coefficient [34].

                      • 	 - 1.8 for viral diseases.
                      • 	 - 1.5 for fungal or bacterial diseases.
                      • 	 - 1.3 for pest-related issues.

                   This adaptive equation ensures sensitivity to microclimatic fluctuations, enabling more
                   realistic modeling of potential disease propagation. The resulting buffer zones were
                   automatically visualized on the GIS interface as circular regions surrounding each
                   infected site, providing an intuitive and actionable tool for hotspot detection and early
                   intervention.
                     Since the PlantVillage dataset does not include spatial metadata, representative coordi-
                   nates corresponding to major tomato and potato production areas in Egypt (e.g., Beheira
                   governorate) were assigned based on national agricultural data and expert consultation.
                   These coordinates reflect actual agricultural regions but were used only to demonstrate
                   GIS integration rather than precise sampling locations.
                     The coefficients (α, β, γ) in Eq. (1) were derived from established epidemiological stud-
                   ies [31–34] and were empirically fine-tuned to match realistic environmental conditions
                   observed in Egypt. A sensitivity analysis with ± 20% parameter variation revealed that
                   β (humidity) had the strongest influence on the predicted spread radius, confirming its
                   dominant role in foliar disease epidemiology.

                     The current implementation integrates live weather data with static GIS layers (soil
                   type, NDVI, and land use), achieving a balance between adaptability and reproducibility.
                   Future work will focus on integrating dynamic field-level observations to further refine
                   the real-time predictive capacity of the GIS-based monitoring system.
                     Model Validation:
                     To validate the predictive equation, the estimated spread radii were compared with
                   ranges reported in previous epidemiological and modeling studies for similar crop–
                   pathogen systems. Specifically, the predicted infection radii (4–9 km for fungal and bac-
                   terial diseases, and 6–11 km for viral diseases under humid and windy conditions) were

                   6.3 Practical impact of GIS integration
                   This study demonstrates the integration of deep learning-based plant disease diagnosis
                   with GIS for real-time spatial monitoring of tomato and potato crops. Each classified leaf
                   image is geotagged and visualized on an interactive web map using Leaflet.js, enabling
                   timely detection and tracking of disease outbreaks (Figs. 8 and 9).
                     The GIS module provides:
                     Crop-specific layers for tomato and potato diseases,
                     Heatmaps highlighting high-density disease zones,
                     Predictive spread buffers based on environmental parameters such as temperature,
                   humidity, and wind speed (see Sect. 3.10.4).
                     The bilingual interface (Arabic and English) delivers both diagnosis and treatment rec-
                   ommendations, enhancing accessibility and adoption among local farmers.
                     Among the evaluated models, DenseNet169 demonstrated the highest accuracy and
                   reliability, making it the most suitable for deployment when computational resources are
                   available. MobileNetV2, while slightly less accurate, offers a practical trade-off between
                   performance and efficiency, enabling real-time GIS-based monitoring even in resource-
                   constrained environments. Custom-CNN, though achieving lower performance,
                   remains valuable for interpretability, experimental benchmarking, and as a baseline for
                   future model development.
                     Overall, the integration of robust models (DenseNet169 and MobileNetV2) with GIS
                   visualization enables early warning, informed decision-making, and proactive inter-
                   ventions, thereby supporting sustainable crop management in real-world agricultural
                   settings.
                     To ensure practical validation, the GIS-integrated diagnostic web system was locally
                   deployed for functional testing. Through this implementation, all modules—including
                   spatial visualization, environmental data integration, and disease spread prediction—
                   were successfully evaluated in real time. Although the current system operates in a local
                   environment, it demonstrates full operational capability and can be readily extended to
                   an online deployment in future work.

                   6.4 Comparative analysis of deep learning models
                   A comparative evaluation was conducted on three deep learning architectures using the
                   same 13-class PlantVillage subset, each trained under identical conditions for 40 epochs.
                     DenseNet169 achieved the highest classification accuracy of 98.24%, confirming the
                   strength of deep transfer learning and its ability to capture complex hierarchical repre-
                   sentations for fine-grained plant disease classification.
Maged et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence   (2026) 6:344                                                      Page 28 of 33




                      MobileNetV2 attained 94.66% accuracy, providing a strong balance between accuracy
                   and computational efficiency. Its lightweight nature makes it particularly well-suited for
                   real-time GIS-based deployment in agricultural settings where resource constraints are
                   a major concern.
                      In contrast, the Custom-CNN model, built and trained from scratch, achieved 82.60%
                   accuracy. While its performance was lower than the transfer learning models, it estab-
                   lishes an important baseline and demonstrates the limitations of shallow architectures
                   when applied to complex agricultural datasets without pre-trained knowledge.
                      This comparative assessment highlights a clear trade-off: transfer learning–based
                   models (DenseNet169 and MobileNetV2) significantly outperform models trained from
                   scratch, yet MobileNetV2 retains an edge in deployability due to its lightweight design.
                   Meanwhile, DenseNet169 sets the benchmark for maximum achievable accuracy in this
                   study, albeit with higher computational cost.

                   models, emphasizing the balance between classification accuracy, computational effi-
                   ciency, and practical deployability in precision agriculture.
                      The comparative analysis demonstrates that DenseNet169 achieved the highest accu-
                   racy (98.24%), reflecting its superior ability to capture fine-grained disease features.
                   MobileNetV2 achieved 94.66%, offering a strong trade-off between accuracy, stability,
                   and computational efficiency, while the Custom-CNN reached 82.60%, highlighting the
                   limitations of training models from scratch without leveraging transfer learning.
                      Although MobileNetV2 provided a more lightweight and efficient option,
                   DenseNet169 was ultimately selected for deployment due to its superior diagnos-
                   tic precision and robustness across all disease classes. This higher accuracy makes
                   DenseNet169 particularly reliable for real-world agricultural scenarios where mini-
                   mizing misclassification is critical. Despite its heavier computational cost compared to
                   MobileNetV2, the performance gains of DenseNet169 justify its use, especially in a GIS-
                   enabled framework where diagnostic reliability is prioritized over inference speed.
                      Overall, DenseNet169 represents the best compromise between accuracy and practical
                   applicability in this study. By experimenting with three distinct models—DenseNet169,
                   MobileNetV2, and Custom-CNN—the evaluation clearly showed that DenseNet169
                   provides the most reliable diagnostic outcomes. Its integration with the GIS module
                   enables precise geospatial disease mapping, early outbreak detection, and predictive
                   spread analysis, thereby enhancing the system’s value as a decision-support tool for sus-
                   tainable crop management.

                   6.5 GIS integration process
                   The integration of Geographic Information Systems (GIS) into the plant disease diagno-
                   sis pipeline establishes a spatial–temporal framework for enhanced monitoring, analy-
                   sis, and prediction. Once a disease is classified by the DenseNet169 model, the system
                   automatically geotags the result, records the timestamp, and plots each diagnosis on an
                   interactive Leaflet-based map. Diagnoses are categorized by crop type and disease class,
                   while layered views for tomato and potato, combined with filters for disease type, sever-
                   ity, and date, allow users to visualize outbreaks, detect spatial clustering, and track dis-
                   ease progression over time.
Maged et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence   (2026) 6:344                                                      Page 29 of 33




                     A key feature of the system is the integration of a disease risk prediction module that
                   computes localized risk scores based on environmental parameters, including tempera-
                   ture, humidity, and wind speed, retrieved through the OpenWeatherMap API. Following
                   the formulation in Eq. (1), these risk scores are visualized as a dynamic heatmap over-
                   lay on the GIS interface, where warmer colors denote high-risk zones and cooler colors
                   indicate lower risk areas. This predictive visualization facilitates early intervention and
                   supports proactive management of potential outbreaks.
                     In addition, the GIS module provides downloadable CSV datasets that include disease
                   diagnosis records (class, coordinates, date, and confidence level), real-time weather data,
                   and an optional merged file for customized offline analysis. By combining the high diag-
                   nostic precision of DenseNet169 with environmental integration and interactive geospa-
                   tial visualization, the GIS component functions as a robust decision-support tool. This
                   empowers stakeholders to monitor ongoing outbreaks, anticipate future risks, and opti-
                   mize resource allocation in precision agriculture, thereby advancing sustainable crop
                   management.

                   6.6 Diagnosis logging and decision support features
                   The system automatically records each diagnosis generated by the DenseNet169 model,
                   including the timestamp, predicted disease, crop type, and geographic location. These
                   records are stored in a downloadable CSV file, enabling users to track disease progres-
                   sion over time and conduct offline analyses. Furthermore, crop-specific GIS layers for
                   tomato and potato facilitate spatial visualization of disease distribution, where each
                   interactive marker provides detailed metadata for real-time, location-aware monitoring.
                   To enhance interpretability and trust, Grad-CAM visualizations are integrated, high-
                   lighting the image regions that most influenced the model’s predictions.
                      Beyond image-based diagnostics, the framework incorporates real-time environmen-
                   tal data from the OpenWeatherMap API, providing contextual insights that strengthen
                   proactive disease management. By uniting automated logging, explainable AI tech-
                   niques, and environmental data integration, the system operates as a comprehensive
                   decision-support platform. This empowers farmers, agronomists, and policymakers to
                   implement timely, evidence-based interventions, minimizing yield losses and promoting
                   long-term agricultural sustainability.
                      In future implementations, access to diagnosis logs and downloadable datasets may
                   be restricted to administrators only, ensuring that sensitive data is centrally analyzed to
                   support more strategic decision-making and improve disease management policies at
                   the regional or national level.

                   7 Limitations
                   Although the system achieved high classification accuracy and showed strong potential
                   for practical use, several limitations remain. First, the training process relied solely on
                   the PlantVillage dataset, which was collected under controlled experimental settings.
                   This reliance may limit the model’s ability to generalize effectively to real-world agricul-
                   tural fields, where images often contain complex backgrounds, varying illumination, leaf
                   occlusions, and environmental noise.
                     Second, the model is restricted to 13 predefined disease classes. It does not support
                   open-set recognition for previously unseen diseases or multi-label detection when
Maged et al. Discover Artificial Intelligence   (2026) 6:344                                                      Page 30 of 33




                   multiple infections occur simultaneously. Moreover, class imbalance within the dataset
                   could reduce sensitivity toward underrepresented diseases, lowering detection reliability.
                     Third, the GIS component currently relies on predefined coordinates instead of real-
                   time GPS input, which restricts the precision of outbreak mapping. Additionally, the
                   system requires stable internet connectivity to retrieve live weather data and generate
                   real-time GIS visualizations, potentially limiting usability in remote or low-connectivity
                   areas.
                     Taken together, these limitations affect the system’s generalizability, real-time applica-
                   bility, and scalability for adoption in diverse agricultural contexts.

                   8 Future work
                   Future research and development could focus on several key enhancements. Integration
                   of time-series satellite imagery and vegetation indices such as NDVI may enable early
                   detection of plant stress and yield forecasting. Developing an offline-capable mobile
                   application would also support deployment in low-connectivity regions, ensuring acces-
                   sibility for farmers in rural areas.
                     Expanding multilingual support is another promising direction, allowing the system
                   to serve diverse farming communities across different regions. Incorporating explain-
                   able AI techniques could improve model interpretability and farmer trust, while auto-
                   mated alert systems triggered by weather conditions or spatial clustering of cases could
                   strengthen disease management responses.
                     Furthermore, combining the platform with historical climate records may enhance
                   predictive modeling of disease spread, while linking it to economic analysis modules
                   could help quantify the financial impact of crop diseases on productivity and market
                   dynamics. Collectively, these improvements aim to enhance robustness, accessibility,
                   and practical relevance, transforming the system into a comprehensive decision-support
                   framework for precision agriculture.

                   9 Conclusion
                   This study presented an integrated framework that combines deep learning with Geo-
                   graphic Information Systems (GIS) to enable accurate diagnosis and spatial moni-
                   toring of tomato and potato diseases. Three models were comparatively evaluated:
                   DenseNet169 (98.24% accuracy), MobileNetV2 (94.66%), and a Custom-CNN baseline
                   (82.60%). The results highlight the clear superiority of transfer learning approaches, with
                   DenseNet169 achieving the highest performance, while the Custom-CNN underscored
                   the limitations of shallow architectures trained from scratch. Importantly, the best-per-
                   forming model (DenseNet169) was selected and fully integrated with the GIS module,
                   ensuring that the most accurate predictions are directly leveraged for spatial analysis
                   and decision support.
                      In addition to classification, the system provides a bilingual web interface (Arabic/Eng-
                   lish) enriched with Grad-CAM visualizations, enhancing interpretability and building
                   user trust. Disease predictions are geotagged and integrated into a GIS module, enabling
                   spatial visualization, environmental data integration, and predictive analysis of potential
                   disease outbreaks. Validation in Beheira, Egypt, showed strong alignment with expert
                   assessments, confirming the system’s robustness and practical utility in real-world agri-
                   cultural settings.

                     Beyond its technical contributions, the proposed system also demonstrates substan-
                   tial practical and economic benefits. Early and accurate diagnosis enables farmers to
                   minimize unnecessary pesticide use, thereby reducing production costs and mitigating
                   environmental impact. By preventing large-scale disease outbreaks, the framework helps
                   safeguard crop yield and stabilize market supply. Moreover, GIS integration supports
                   agricultural authorities in optimizing resource allocation by targeting high-risk zones
                   with tailored interventions, reducing both operational costs and response time. Collec-
                   tively, these outcomes emphasize the system’s role not only as a technological innovation
                   but also as an economically sustainable solution for precision agriculture.
                     Future work will focus on extending coverage to additional crops and disease types,
                   developing offline-capable mobile applications to support farmers in low-connectivity
                   regions, and strengthening collaboration with agricultural authorities to facilitate large-
                   scale adoption. Overall, this research demonstrates the transformative potential of
                   integrating AI-based diagnostics with geospatial intelligence, providing a scalable and
                   sustainable decision-support system for precision agriculture.
